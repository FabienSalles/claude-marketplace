# Bullet formulas and the human read

## 1. The two readers

This governs everything below. Widely quoted eye-tracking work measures roughly **seven
seconds** of initial scan. State its limits whenever the figure is used: about thirty
recruiters, never peer-reviewed, published by a company with a commercial interest in the
result. Treat the seven seconds as a **rejection time, not a reading time**. Documents that
survive the scan get **one and a half to four minutes**.

So the document serves two readers in sequence, with opposite needs:

| Reader | Needs | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| The scanner (seconds) | Hierarchy, line starts, figures in place | Everything looks equally important |
| The evaluator (minutes) | Substance, named technical objects, proof | Lines are polished and empty |

Optimising for one loses the other. Every rule below is a compromise between them, not a
preference.

## 2. The six fixation points

The scan lands on six things. They must be legible without effort in the **top third of
page one**:

1. name
2. current job title
3. current company
4. employment dates
5. previous title and company
6. education

**Contractor-specific consequence.** When the "current company" is the person's own
structure, that field tells the reader nothing. Append the client or the sector so the
fixation point carries information.

## 3. The one formula to write

Synthesis of the sources in section 4, one formula:

> **Verb in first position → object with the technology named → quantified result or scope.**
> All on the SAME line.

The reader scans line starts, so the verb leads. The figure never spills to the next line:
a result that wraps is a result the scanner does not see.

**Tech variant.** Prefix the bullet with a short scannable summary, then a colon, then the
action and the quantified result. The prefix is what the eye catches during the initial
scan, and the rest is what the evaluator reads.

**Quantification form.** In the XYZ author's own examples, percentages are routinely paired
with an absolute value in parentheses. Follow that: a percentage alone has no scale.

## 4. Where the formulas come from

Several of these are commonly misattributed. Be precise about origins.

| Formula | Origin and status | Take | Leave |
|---|---|---|---|
| **XYZ** — "Accomplished [X] as measured by [Y], by doing [Z]" | A LinkedIn post by Google's then SVP of People Operations. **Not** an internal Google document. | The measurement clause; percentage plus absolute value | The three-clause sentence shape, too long for a scanned line |
| **STAR** — Situation/Task, Action, Result | Created in 1974 by a talent-assessment firm as a **behavioural interview protocol**. Its official form has **three** blocks, not four. The UK national careers service does also recommend it for CVs. | Use it for interview preparation | Do not transpose it literally into a bullet |
| **CAR** — Challenge, Action, Result | The documented fallback when no metric exists. | The explicit challenge as a substitute for a figure | — |
| **PAR** — Project, Action, Result | Taught by MIT's careers office. | Its rarely quoted and very tech-relevant instruction: state **how** the task was performed, not just what was done. If software was written, say in what language. | — |
| **Action + Project + Result** | Taught by Yale. | Quantify with **baseline and target**, not only a delta. | — |

**The STAR tension, stated rather than smoothed over:** transposed literally into a bullet,
STAR spends most of the line on context and reaches the result last, which is the opposite
of how a line is scanned. Verdict: STAR belongs in interview preparation; use its
compressed descendants (CAR, PAR) in the document.

## 5. No metric available

Switch to CAR and **make the challenge explicit**, so the bullet still carries weight
without a figure.

A bullet with **neither a figure nor a stated challenge should be cut.** It costs surface
and proves nothing.

## 6. Anti-markers

The practical core of this file. Reviewers name three things that make a document read as
generated: **unnatural phrasing**, **repetitive or generic language**, and **vague or
inflated descriptions**.

Critical framing, do not drop it: **these are not markers of AI, they are markers of bad
writing.** A generic human document triggers them identically. The countermeasure is
therefore the same as it has always been, specificity.

| Marker | Test / fix |
|---|---|
| **Keyword saturation** (reviewers name this FIRST) | Every single term from the posting appears. It reads as regurgitation. Select, do not mirror. |
| Saturated verbs | leveraged, spearheaded, orchestrated, synergized, championed, drove, owned. In French: piloté, orchestré, impulsé. Replace with the verb of the actual act. |
| Vocabulary tells | delve, tapestry, meticulous. |
| Identical rhythm across bullets | Every line the same length, the same action-plus-result shape. **Vary length deliberately.** |
| Claim with no named technical object | If no tool, language, system or volume is named, it is not a claim. |
| Figure with no anchor | "increased revenue by 200%" with no period, no baseline, no scope. Anchor or delete. |
| Skill inflation | Expert-level claims across an implausible number of platforms. |
| Authority adjectives with no demonstration | An adjective that no bullet proves is noise. |
| Em dashes mid-sentence | Two short sentences, or parentheses. |

## 7. Rule of credibility

**Every technology listed in the skills section must appear in at least one engagement
bullet, with a figure or a context.**

A skill listed and never demonstrated is what reviewers call inflated duties. This rule
makes the skills section auditable in one pass: any orphan entry is either promoted into a
bullet or removed.

## 8. Tolerance data

To calibrate, not to panic:

| Measure | Figure |
|---|---|
| Hiring managers who would reject a document they believe fully machine-generated | ~1 in 5 |
| Who find machine assistance acceptable for drafting and proofreading | ~half |
| Who reject a machine-written document that is **not personalised** | ~3 in 5 |
| Who value personalised detail over polished phrasing | a large majority |

Conclusion to state: **what is sanctioned is genericness, not the tool.** And no major ATS
detects authorship, so the risk is entirely human, at reading time.

## 9. Jargon

Translate client-internal vocabulary into market vocabulary. An internal project name, an
in-house acronym or a client-specific job title matches no posting keyword and means
nothing outside the client.

- Replace with the generic function.
- Keep the internal name in parentheses at most.
- **Test:** have someone outside the domain read it and point at everything they do not
  understand.

## 10. Spelling

A blocking pass before any send. This is the single most cited immediate rejection reason,
around **three quarters of recruiters**.

The criterion's relevance is contested by practitioners, and that contestation is honest to
report. It changes nothing operationally: the cost of proofreading is nil and the cost of
rejection is total.

## 11. Skills section form

Grouped by category, plain text separated by pipes or bullets.

**Never** progress bars, stars, percentages or graphical levels. Three reasons: they do not
parse, colour carries no information after extraction, and they consume surface at the
price of a keyword. A textual level in parentheses only if the posting asks for one.

**On how many skills to list, the sources diverge** and the divergence is real: 6 to 12 in
one place, 10 to 20 in another, from the same publisher. Refuse to fix a number. The stable
rule is the one that survives both: every listed skill is drawn from the posting's
vocabulary and backed by a bullet (section 7).

## 12. Length

Two source types, different natures, both worth citing with their nature attached.

| Source | Nature | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Simulation study, n=482 recruiters, 7712 documents, publisher commercially interested | Behavioural | Two pages preferred **2.3x** over one; **2.9x** for managerial roles |
| Later declarative survey, same publisher | Declarative | Most recruiters recommend one page **in the abstract** |
| Same survey | Declarative | **45%** place the two-page threshold at ten years of experience |
| Same survey | Declarative | **59%** judge more than two pages excessive at any level |
| Same survey | Declarative | **89%** consider missing information worse than excessive detail |
| Same survey | Declarative | **84%** recommend adjusting content to avoid half-empty pages |

**Verdict:** beyond ten years of experience, two **full** pages. Never three. Never compress
legibility to hit one page. No one-and-a-half-page documents.

**Sectoral exception, to arbitrate explicitly rather than smooth over:** the FAANG / IC tech
norm is one page, which contradicts the above. When the target is that norm, say so and
choose deliberately; do not let the contradiction sit unresolved in the document.

## 13. Gaps

Declare every gap with a **one-line reason**. Measured on a large real-application field
experiment:

| Situation | Callback effect |
|---|---|
| Unexplained gap | costs about **45%** of callbacks |
| A reason provided | recovers roughly **58%** (4.3% to 6.8%) |
| Reason is training or upskilling | best performer, **8.5%** |

The cliff sits between **two and three years** of gap.

**Never mask a gap** by switching to year-only dates or a functional format. The scan looks
for dates and progression (section 2), so a format that removes them removes exactly what
the reader is looking for.
